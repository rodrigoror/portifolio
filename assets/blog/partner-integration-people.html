Integration Is About People:
Eight Years of Fintech Partners, From Dock to Celcoin

Rodrigo Ramos · November 2026

1. A Bank Is a Set of Integrations

When I joined alt.bank's founding team at the end of 2018, I thought I was going to build a banking app. What I actually spent the next eight years doing was integrating. A digital bank doesn't issue cards, hold accounts, print boletos or lend money on its own. Partners do those things, and the bank connects them into one experience the customer can trust.

Over those years I worked with four partners: Dock, FIS, Pismo and Celcoin. I worked with them first as a developer, then as a tech lead, and later as the engineering manager and architect responsible for the product. Each one taught me something different. This isn't a ranking. Each partner was the right choice for the moment the company was in, and each integration shaped the next one.

I'll tell the story from two points of view at once: the architect, who asks "how does this fail, and what happens then?", and the product manager, who asks "what does the customer feel, and what does it cost us?". Every integration needs both questions.

The core idea: the code in an integration is the easy part, and with AI it's getting easier every year. The hard part is aligning people from different companies, with different incentives, around one contract and one definition of "correct".

2. Dock, 2019: Learning to Distrust a 200 OK

Our first card ran on Dock's Banking-as-a-Service platform. It was a debit card that worked almost like a prepaid credit card: the customer loaded the account and spent from it, and Dock processed it all.

It was 2019, with no AI assistant to read the documentation for us. I read it myself, end to end. Then I tested every endpoint and every combination of the integration, because the documentation tells you what should happen and only a test tells you what does.

The Errors That Don't Look Like Errors

Every monitoring setup catches a 401 or a 5xx, and those were never our problem. The problem was the 200 OK that was wrong: a status value we had never mapped, a field that changed format, a contract that shifted without any notice in the response. The HTTP layer said "success", and the business was quietly getting the wrong answer.

So we built monitoring for meaning, not for transport. In Prometheus, we created events that counted the responses our code couldn't interpret: unknown values, missing fields, responses that broke the contract we had agreed. We grouped them by endpoint and field, and alerted on them like errors, because they were errors.

# Illustrative: count what the HTTP status can't tell you
partner_response_unmapped_total{endpoint="card_status", field="status"}
partner_response_contract_violation_total{endpoint="transactions", reason="missing_field"}

Those metrics changed how I design integrations. Every response from a partner gets validated against the contract before it reaches the domain. Anything we don't recognize falls into an explicit "unknown" path that is logged, counted and safe, instead of crashing or, worse, being treated as valid.

Two points of view

Architect: a partner's response is input, and input gets validated. Design every adapter to fail safe on what it doesn't understand.
Product manager: a silent wrong answer is worse than an outage. An outage generates a ticket; a wrong balance generates a customer who stops trusting the bank.

It was a hard time, and it taught me more about resilience than anything else in my career. Since then, I start every integration by assuming it will fail, and I prepare the system for that.

3. FIS: Learning How Billing Really Works

The next step was the company's biggest bet: our own credit card. The world already knew how to run credit cards, so we didn't reinvent the processor. We integrated with FIS and Visa, and I led the technical side of the move from Banking-as-a-Service to our own card.

FIS brought decades of card processing. Its integration model came from a different era of banking, though, built on file exchange, not real-time APIs. To get from a transaction to a paid bill, we wrote file readers for every step: the invoice, the boleto, the barcode. Much of the exchange ran on CNAB files, the Brazilian banking standard for batch files.

When the Contract Is a File

File-based integration has a different failure profile from APIs. A file can arrive late, arrive incomplete, or not arrive at all, and you find out hours after the fact. Reconciling in real time was very hard, because the source of truth only showed up in batches.

That's where I learned how billing customers really works: cycles, invoices, due dates, payment slips, payment confirmations. Most of all, I learned how to keep collecting even when the files fail or arrive late: what the system does while it waits, what it tells the customer, and how it catches up without charging twice or missing a payment.

The batch trap: a customer lives in real time. If the payment they made this morning only shows up after tomorrow's file, your product has a gap the customer can see. Design for the gap explicitly, or your support team will do it for you, one ticket at a time.
Two points of view

Architect: with batch partners, idempotency and reconciliation are part of the design from day one. Every file must be safe to process twice and easy to compare.
Product manager: learn the business process behind the integration (billing, collection, chargebacks) before you design the screens. The integration model limits the product you can offer.

4. Pismo: Migrating the Engine

The file-based model worked, but it didn't let us grow the way we wanted. Every new product idea ran into the same batch cycle. So we decided to migrate the card processing to Pismo.

The hard part was moving the engine: collection, billing and customer transactions, the processes that run every day and that customers feel immediately when they break.

We moved 100,000 credit-card customers with zero downtime and zero refused transactions. I led 10 people (8 developers and 2 QAs) and coordinated around 20 more from Security, DevOps, Pismo, Dock, Celcoin and Unico. The migration app was designed for doubt: field mapping, reconciliation in shadow mode, and a rollback at every stage.

Pismo made a hard project easier. Its interface was clear, its documentation was good, and its team was genuinely able and willing to help. When a partner's engineers sit at the same table as yours, problems get solved in a call instead of a ticket queue.

The work was split across people as much as across systems. The architect I paired with owned the systems integration; I owned the data integration. Neither half would have worked without the other, and neither of us could have done it without the partner teams.

Two points of view

Architect: in a migration, run the old and new paths side by side and compare them before you switch. Rollback is a feature you build, not a hope.
Product manager: the success criterion is what the customer doesn't notice. Zero refused transactions was the real requirement; everything else served it.

5. Celcoin: The First AI-First Integration

When our contract with Dock ended, the accounts moved to Celcoin. This was the first integration we did AI-first, with Claude in place from the start.

The work that took me weeks in 2019 changed shape. Claude read the documentation and generated the new connection interface. That didn't remove the engineering; it moved it. Our job became tests, tests and more tests. When code is generated, validation is the key: you're no longer checking whether someone typed it right, you're checking whether it does what the contract and the business need.

We integrated Celcoin's BaaS and LaaS APIs: 20 customer journeys in one week, with 80% passing the first automated run.

LaaS: When Code Is Generated, Not Written

The latest integration was lending-as-a-service: Celcoin's LaaS, with QI Tech's LaaS alongside it as a complement, both integrated with AI. It was the easiest integration I've done. Claude did the analysis, and a well-built MCP server gave it access to what it needed. All the information was in the context. Every formula was defined before the first line of code. The code was generated, not coded. The resilience unit tests were there from the start, and so were the abstractions.

That last point is the Dock lesson paying off years later. One rule I hold to: the partner's name doesn't go into our database. Our domain talks about accounts, cards, loans and payments, not about a vendor. The partner name lives only in the adapter, or in a service whose only job is to integrate with that partner.

# Illustrative
loan.provider_reference      -- our domain: a loan, with an external reference
celcoin_loan_id              -- avoid: the vendor leaks into the model

It isn't about any specific partner. It's what makes it possible to change partners, or add a second one, without rewriting the core. It's also why AI could generate the new connectors so quickly: the abstraction was already there, waiting for an implementation.

Two points of view

Architect: AI makes connectors cheap, so the value moves to the contract, the abstraction and the tests. Invest there.
Product manager: faster integration means the partner choice becomes a business decision again, not a technical lock-in. That's leverage in every negotiation.

6. What Changed and What Didn't

Dock (2019) FIS Pismo Celcoin and QI Tech
What we integrated BaaS debit card Own credit card: billing, boleto, barcode Card processing: collection, billing, transactions Accounts and lending (Celcoin BaaS and LaaS), with QI Tech LaaS as a complement
Contract style APIs Files (CNAB) APIs APIs
How we built it Read every page, test every endpoint File readers and reconciliation Migration app, shadow mode, rollback AI-generated connectors, test-heavy validation
The main lesson A 200 can be wrong Keep collecting when files fail Move the engine without stopping it Validation is the work

The tools changed completely in eight years. In 2019, every integration started with me reading documentation. Today it starts with an agent reading it. What didn't change is what decides whether an integration succeeds in production:

  • An explicit contract, validated on every response.
  • A domain that belongs to us, with partners behind adapters.
  • Monitoring for meaning, not just for status codes.
  • A plan for failure: late files, unknown values, rollbacks.
  • People who trust each other on both sides of the contract.

7. Integration Is Between People

Looking back at all four, the clearest lesson is that the work of a developer, an architect or a product manager isn't really about code. It's about integration, and integration happens between people before it happens between systems.

Every person we talked to and worked with left something in the code we delivered: the partner engineer who explained what a field really meant, the security team that changed a flow, the operations analyst who knew which file always arrived late, the QA who found the case nobody had mapped. No single person wrote those integrations, and that's exactly why they worked.

Each side of an integration has its own interests. The partner wants a stable contract and predictable volume. Security wants control. Operations wants fewer surprises. Business wants the launch date. The customer wants none of it to be visible. Managing an integration means making those interests explicit and finding the design where they line up:

  • Agree on what "correct" means, in writing, before anyone builds. Most integration bugs are two companies with two definitions of the same word.
  • Share the monitoring. When both sides see the same contract violations, the conversation moves from blame to fixing.
  • Bring partner engineers into the room. The best moments of every integration I've done were the ones where their people and ours worked as one team.
  • Protect the customer from the seams. Whatever happens between companies, the customer should see one bank.
The takeaway: AI now writes most of the connector. What it can't do is align four companies on one definition of a successful transaction. That alignment is the real job of the architect and the product manager, and it's what puts a high-quality system in production.

Assume the system will fail. Keep your domain your own. And remember that every integration is a relationship before it's an API.
